📐 Statistical Analysis

📝 What Does This Mean?

📖 Glossary of Terms

Trend

A long-term increase or decrease in a measured variable over time. A trend indicates a consistent directional change, not just random fluctuations.

Statistical Significance

A measure of confidence that an observed trend is real and not just due to random chance. A significance level (α) of 0.05 means there is less than a 5% probability the trend occurred by chance.

p-value

The probability of observing a trend as strong as (or stronger than) the one found, assuming no real trend exists. Smaller p-values indicate stronger evidence for a real trend. Typically, p < 0.05 is considered significant.

Linear Regression

A statistical method that fits a straight line through the data to estimate the rate of change. The slope of this line represents the average rate of change per time step.

R² (R-squared)

The fraction of the data's variation explained by the linear trend. R² = 0 means the trend explains nothing; R² = 1 means the trend explains all variation. For environmental data with seasons, R² for trends is often small.

Mann-Kendall Test

A non-parametric statistical test that determines whether a series of values tends to increase or decrease over time. It doesn't require the data to follow a normal distribution, making it well-suited for environmental data.

Kendall's τ (tau)

A measure of the strength of the trend from the Mann-Kendall test. Ranges from -1 (perfect decreasing trend) to +1 (perfect increasing trend). Values near 0 indicate no trend.

Sen's Slope

The median of all pairwise slopes between data points. It is more robust to outliers than ordinary regression and provides a reliable estimate of the rate of change.

Confidence Interval (CI)

A range of values within which the true slope likely falls. A 95% CI means we are 95% confident the real trend is within this range. If the CI doesn't include zero, the trend is significant.

Anomaly

The deviation of a measurement from the long-term average for that time of year. Anomalies remove the seasonal cycle, making it easier to spot trends.

Seasonal Decomposition

Separating a time series into three components: the long-term trend, the repeating seasonal cycle, and the remaining residual (random variation).

Moving Average

A smoothing technique that averages values within a sliding window (e.g., 12 months). It reduces noise and helps reveal the underlying trend.

Deseasonalized Data

Data with the seasonal cycle removed. This makes it easier to see the long-term trend without seasonal ups and downs.

NASA POWER

The Prediction Of Worldwide Energy Resources project provides solar and meteorological data from NASA research for renewable energy, agriculture, and sustainability. Data is derived from satellite observations and models including MERRA-2 and GLDAS.
